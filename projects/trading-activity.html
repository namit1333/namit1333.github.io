Does heavy trading predict prices?

We tested a popular market hunch against real data. Trading activity tells you the market is about to move — it does not tell you which way.

Read the full study ↗ ← Back to all projects

The question

When trading is unusually heavy today, does that tell you anything about tomorrow? It’s one of the oldest hunches in markets: a rush of buying and selling must mean something is coming.

The question isn’t whether it feels true. The question is whether it survives testing — in different markets, over different years, and under checks designed to make it fail.

What we did

  1. Two very different markets.A slice of the S&P 500 — slow, institutional — and Bitcoin, which never sleeps and swings wildly. A pattern appearing in both is harder to dismiss as a fluke.
  2. Years of data, hour by hour.Roughly thirteen thousand hourly readings, plus five years of daily ones.
  3. The expectations came first.We wrote down what we expected to find before looking, so the result couldn’t be reverse-engineered after the fact.
  4. We tried hard to prove ourselves wrong.Shuffle the data thousands of times and re-measure. Re-draw the sample and re-fit. Remove one period at a time. Try many settings. Each check asks the same question: could this just be luck?

What we found

Supported

Heavy activity → bigger moves

The busiest periods are followed by larger price swings — in both markets, at both speeds, in every slice of time we tested. The relationship survived every reliability check.

Supported

Heavy activity → more wobble

Volatility after busy periods is higher. This was the most stable relationship in the study.

Not supported

Heavy activity → direction

Volume did not predict up versus down, in any test we ran. We kept that result in the report instead of quietly deleting it.

The headline, in plain words

Volume tells you the market is going to move. It does not tell you which way. Anyone who says “trading is heavy, so the price will rise” is going further than the evidence allows.

Why it matters

Market advice is full of confident sentences that were never tested. This project is a demonstration of what testing one looks like: hypotheses written down first, several independent checks, and an honest account of the part that didn’t work.

Who it’s for: anyone curious about markets, and anyone who wants to see what careful, reproducible research looks like — including the negative results.

Good to know

  • This studies association — patterns that travel together — not proof of cause and effect.
  • It is research, not financial advice.
  • The failed prediction is part of the finding, not an embarrassment to hide.

Want to look under the hood?

The full study — methods, charts, and every check — is public on GitHub. No technical background needed to follow the story.
